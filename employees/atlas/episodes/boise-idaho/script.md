GUIDE: March 1865. Lewiston, Idaho Territory, way up in the north. The town has posted an armed guard. Over a seal and a pile of papers. The seal of the territory and its official records. Because whoever holds those holds the capital.
KID: Armed guards over paperwork. That's a real fight.
GUIDE: It was. A few months earlier the territorial legislature had voted to move the capital south, to a little army town called Boise. Lewiston said, no you didn't, and went to court. KID: So who wins?
GUIDE: On March 2nd a new territorial secretary arrives, a man named C. Dewitt Smith. He has money, and he has the power to call federal troops. One afternoon he goes for a ride. KID: They jailed the paperwork.
GUIDE: They jailed the paperwork. Smith rode into Boise on April 14th, 1865. And that's how the city you're driving to became the capital of Idaho. Not by a grand plan. By gold, by votes, and by a man who went for a ride.

GUIDE: That's the plan. Where Idaho came from, why Boise grew, who lives there, where the money is, especially in construction, and what to watch for the next twenty-five years. And G, you're in the truck too, so listen for a few things just for you.

KID: Start at the very beginning. What's the land like?
GUIDE: Picture southern Idaho as a wide, flat bowl of old lava rock and river sand. That's the Snake River Plain. The Boise River comes out of the mountains heading west, and right where it leaves the foothills and spreads onto the flat, that's where Boise sits. Mountains behind, high desert in front, a river through the middle. Everything about the town follows from that river.
KID: Any big events in the geology?
GUIDE: One wild one. About seventeen thousand years ago, an ice-age lake in what's now Utah, Lake Bonneville, broke through a natural dam. At its peak the flood moved about thirty-three million cubic feet of water a second, at up to seventy miles an hour. The top three hundred fifty feet of a lake covering about thirty-two thousand square miles poured out. It carved the Snake River Canyon and scattered big rounded rocks across the plain that people still call melon boulders.
KID: So the flood made Boise?
GUIDE: No, and that's a good thing to get right. Boise sits on a side river, and nobody's shown the flood shaped the Boise valley itself. It shaped the Snake. Boise was shaped by the Boise River.
KID: And the first people?
GUIDE: Here long before anyone farmed. A site at Cooper's Ferry in western Idaho points to people in this country fifteen to sixteen thousand years ago. The Boise valley itself was Northern Shoshone and Northern Paiute land. Shoshone tradition tells of a yearly gathering where the Boise, the Payette and the Weiser rivers meet the Snake, just west of today's Boise. It opened the fishing season, and people traded shells, hides, dried meat and arrowheads.
KID: So the valley was a trading fair long before it was a farm town.
GUIDE: Long before. Then comes the hard part, and I'll tell it plainly. Gold rushes from the early 1860s brought thousands of settlers into Native land. Raids and retaliation followed, and from 1864 to 1868 the army fought what's called the Snake War against Northern Paiute, Bannock and Shoshone bands, across what's now Oregon, Nevada, California and Idaho. About seventeen hundred and sixty men were killed, wounded or captured. A treaty was signed at Fort Boise in 1864, but the U.S. Senate never ratified it.
KID: What happened to the people who lived in the Boise valley?
GUIDE: In 1867 the government set aside the Fort Hall Reservation, and in 1869 the army moved the Shoshone and Bannock people out of the Boise area to go there. Some writers call that Idaho's Trail of Tears.
KID: Are the tribes still there today?
GUIDE: Very much so. The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes at Fort Hall have about six thousand enrolled members and an elected council, and a federal appeals court upheld their treaty fishing and hunting rights in 1994. And the Shoshone-Paiute Tribes of Duck Valley, down on the Nevada line, are the other southern Idaho nation.

KID: Back to the gold. When does Boise actually show up?
GUIDE: One stop first. The first Fort Boise was a fur post, built by the Hudson's Bay Company in 1834 near where the Boise River meets the Snake. And much of its staff were Hawaiian workers, a long way from home.
KID: Hawaiians, in the high desert. I didn't see that coming.
GUIDE: The fort helped Oregon Trail families, and something like four hundred thousand people used that trail and its branches. After flood damage, the fort was abandoned in 1854.
KID: Then gold.
GUIDE: Then gold. August 2nd, 1862. A small party led by George Grimes and Moses Splawn found rich gold in the Boise Basin, on what's now Grimes Creek. Grimes was killed within days, by most accounts in a clash with Native people. Then the numbers went wild. By the fall of 1863 the Boise Basin held about sixteen thousand people, and Idaho City had more than six thousand. The state historical society says that made Idaho City bigger than Portland, the largest town in the whole Pacific Northwest.
KID: Bigger than Portland. That's just across the river from where we started this morning.
GUIDE: Picture it at night. More than thirty miners' fires visible from Idaho City. And by 1863 the miners had dug eighty miles of ditches to carry water to their claims. Remember those ditches. They come back.
KID: And where's Boise in all this?
GUIDE: Boise was the supply town. On July 4th, 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, the Union Army built a new Fort Boise, about fifty miles upriver from the old one, right where the Oregon Trail crossed the roads up to the mines. The town grew around the fort, and the army stayed forty-nine years. Where the name comes from, nobody's sure. The favorite story is French-speaking trappers crying "les bois," the woods, at the trees along the river. Nice story. Not proven.
KID: So that's the setup for the capital heist.
GUIDE: Right. Congress created Idaho Territory in March 1863, and at first it was enormous: today's Idaho, Montana and most of Wyoming. The first governor set up in Lewiston, in the north, because that's where the people had been. But gold pulled everyone south. By the second legislature in 1864, the south outnumbered the north almost nineteen thousand to under three thousand.
KID: So the south just outvoted them.
GUIDE: The north tried to have Congress carve off a new northern territory. The south blocked that and made Boise the capital, dated December 24th, 1864. Then came the lawsuit, the duck-hunting governor, the armed guard and Smith's ride. The territorial supreme court later upheld the move. Some reference books say 1866; I'm going with the date from the state historical society.
KID: And statehood?
GUIDE: July 3rd, 1890. Idaho became the forty-third state. Boise itself was still tiny, about twenty-three hundred people. One more piece people forget. Chinese miners and workers were a big part of the gold rush. In 1870 the census counted about seventeen hundred and fifty Chinese residents in Idaho City alone. Boise had a Chinatown too. Hold on to that; it comes back later.
---
KID: You said the ditches come back.
GUIDE: Because here's the one big idea about Boise. It's a desert made green by ditches. The city gets about twelve inches of precipitation a year. That's dry country. Right after the gold rush, all the easy land along the river got farmed. Private companies dug the first big canals, like the Ridenbaugh and the New York Canal.
KID: And when the private money ran out?
GUIDE: That happened all over the West, so in 1894 Congress passed the Carey Act. A state could take up to a million acres of desert and sell it in hundred-sixty-acre lots, watered by approved canal companies. Idaho used it like no other state. About sixty percent of all the Carey Act land ever irrigated in America is in Idaho.
KID: Sixty percent. In one state.
GUIDE: Then the Reclamation Act of 1902 brought in the federal government. Upriver from Boise they built Arrowrock Dam, dedicated in 1915. Three hundred fifty feet tall. For years it was the tallest dam in the world. G, that's about as tall as a thirty-story building, holding back a river in the mountains above your new town.
KID: Why does a 1915 dam matter to someone buying a house this year?
GUIDE: Because the valley's farms, and now its subdivisions, still run on water rights from the 1800s and early 1900s. The water in your tap and the water in the farmer's ditch come out of the same mountain snow. We'll come back to that when we talk about the future.

KID: Okay, the Basques. Everybody says Boise is Basque. Why Boise?
GUIDE: Silver. Basque immigrants were drawn to the silver mines at Silver City. The ones who didn't mine ran sheep and cattle and sold lamb and beef to the miners. Later, from the 1940s to about 1970, a ranchers' association recruited sheepherders straight from Spain.
KID: How many Basques are there now?
GUIDE: Honest answer: the counts don't agree, because the census never separated Basques cleanly. Thousands, and one of the largest Basque communities in the country. What's not in doubt is how central they are. Boise has been a sister city to Gernika since 1990. A former Boise mayor, Dave Bieter, speaks Basque.
KID: Where do I go to see it?
GUIDE: The Basque Block, downtown on Grove Street between Capitol Boulevard and Sixth. And every five years there's a big festival called Jaialdi. The last one was the summer of 2025, the first in ten years, because COVID pushed back the 2020 edition.

KID: When does Boise stop being a small town?
GUIDE: Not for a long time. Boise had about twenty-one thousand people in 1920, and almost exactly the same in 1930. It barely moved between the wars. The war changed things. In 1938 Boise's airport had the longest runway in the country, eighty-eight hundred feet. The Army Air Forces trained B-17 and B-24 bomber crews there, more than six thousand men. It was named Gowen Field in 1941, and the Idaho Air National Guard still flies A-10s from it.
KID: G, keep your eyes open when we're near the airport. A-10s.
GUIDE: There's a hard chapter from the same war, about two hours southeast of Boise. Minidoka. From 1942 to 1945 the government held Japanese American families there, most of them from Oregon, Washington and Alaska. At its peak it held about ninety-four hundred people, and the Park Service remembers more than thirteen thousand who lived there over those years. Eight hundred forty-four men from that camp served in the U.S. military during the war. It's a National Historic Site now, and many of those families stayed in the Treasure Valley afterward to farm.
KID: Worth a visit with the family.
GUIDE: Now the builders. Boise produced companies far bigger than a town its size had any right to. Start with Morrison-Knudsen. Harry Morrison and Morris Knudsen met in 1905 on an Idaho irrigation project. Of course they did. They incorporated in 1923 and became one of the Six Companies that built Hoover Dam. MK helped build the Bay Bridge, the Trans-Alaska Pipeline, missile silos, and more than a hundred dams.
KID: All out of Boise.
GUIDE: All out of Boise. In 1941 about twelve hundred workers, many of them MK men, were captured by Japanese forces on Wake and Midway. MK went bankrupt in 1996, and after a chain of buyouts the last Boise jobs went in 2015. Scout's read is that a good share of the valley's contractor families and older project managers trace back to MK. That's a network worth knowing.
KID: Who else?
GUIDE: J. R. Simplot. Left school after eighth grade, at fourteen. Started out feeding hogs. By World War Two he was the biggest fresh-potato shipper in the country. Then in 1967 he made a handshake deal with Ray Kroc to supply McDonald's with frozen fries. By 2005 he supplied more than half of them. He also bankrolled a young chip company called Micron, and he kept the local ski hill afloat by buying its lifts in 1953 and leasing them back. He died in 2008 at ninety-nine, the oldest billionaire on the Forbes list.
KID: A handshake deal. That's a pillar story right there.
GUIDE: It is. Then Joe Albertson founded Albertsons in Boise in 1939, and it's still headquartered there. It merged with Safeway in 2015, and a much bigger deal with Kroger was blocked by a federal judge in December 2024. Boise Cascade formed in 1957 out of two lumber companies, and today makes engineered wood and distributes building products. Then came tech. Hewlett-Packard bought a hundred-fifty-acre site in Boise in 1973, and in 1978 four men founded Micron, backed by local businessmen and then by Simplot.
KID: Remember Micron.
GUIDE: Remember Micron. It's the center of Boise's next chapter.

KID: You promised the hard chapters, told plainly.
GUIDE: Two from the twentieth century. In 1955, a criminal case involving young men turned into something much bigger. The city's only daily paper ran an editorial headlined "Crush the Monster," and the investigation grew into a hunt for gay men across the city. About fifteen hundred people were questioned. Fifteen men were sentenced, one of them to life in prison, and he served nine years. Within three weeks the paper reversed itself and called for treatment instead of prison, and it all wound down by early 1957. A writer named John Gerassi later argued it was really about who controlled the city. That's his argument, and it's disputed. What isn't disputed is that it still colors how some Boiseans see their own city.
KID: And the second one?
GUIDE: The second is about buildings. Starting in the late 1960s, city leaders wanted a big regional shopping mall downtown. The redevelopment agency tore down a lot of old Boise to make room, including Chinatown, the original City Hall and the Pierce Building. The plan would have taken out whole blocks of historic buildings. The mall never came, and by about 1980 the downtown core was, in one account's words, largely deserted.
KID: How did it turn around?
GUIDE: Citizens pushed back. Preservation Idaho formed in the early seventies to stop the demolitions, and public forums drew more than sixteen hundred people. In the 1983 and 1985 elections, voters chose leaders who opposed the downtown mall and backed a suburban one instead. That became Boise Towne Square, which started construction in late 1986, out in west Boise.
KID: So the big mall on my side of town exists because of a fight over downtown.
GUIDE: Exactly. And the very same years produced one of Boise's best decisions. In 1962 the city's plan called for a continuous public green belt along the river. Three donated parcels started it in 1967, and a 1971 rule pushed new buildings and parking seventy feet back from the water. Today the Greenbelt runs more than twenty miles. Same city, same era. One bad call, one great one.

KID: Now the boom. Give me the curve.
GUIDE: Boise was about thirty-four thousand people in 1950, and still about thirty-four thousand in 1960. Then it more than doubled, to about seventy-five thousand by 1970, likely helped by annexation, though that's unconfirmed. Two hundred thirty-six thousand in 2020. The Census Bureau and the regional planners use different methods and their latest numbers are about eighteen thousand apart.
KID: And the whole valley?
GUIDE: That's the real story. Ada County is Boise, Meridian, Eagle, Kuna and Star. Canyon County next door is Nampa, Caldwell and Middleton. Together they had about seven hundred twenty-six thousand people at the 2020 Census. The regional planners estimate about eight hundred seventy-seven thousand this year. That's about a hundred fifty thousand more people in six years. A whole new Meridian, added.
KID: And Meridian used to be what?
GUIDE: A dairy and orchard town of about ninety-six hundred people in 1990. Now about a hundred fifty-two thousand, the second-largest city in Idaho. Star more than doubled in six years. And statewide, Idaho grew faster than any other state from 2020 to 2025, more than ten percent, and passed two million people.
KID: Where are they all coming from?
GUIDE: Everybody will tell you California, Washington and Oregon. It may well be right, but I couldn't confirm it from a solid source, so treat it as street wisdom. What's solid is this. In 2025 about three quarters of Idaho's growth came from people moving in, and about ninety percent of them came from other states. Growth has cooled, from about three percent a year in 2021 to about one and a half now, but that's still almost three times the national rate.
KID: Housing. I'm about to buy into this.
GUIDE: The solid number is October 2025. The median Ada County home sold for about five hundred fifty thousand dollars, flat on the year. Canyon County, about four hundred twenty-five thousand. You're buying into a market that stopped racing but never went back to the old prices.

GUIDE: Apartments. In a recent count, single-family permits held steady but multifamily permits dropped by more than a third. Scout's read: fewer apartments started now could mean a tighter rental market in a couple of years, right when Micron's workforce peaks.
KID: And where do people work versus where they sleep?
GUIDE: Boise is the job center, and the suburbs are the bedrooms. About forty-four percent of the metro's jobs are in the city of Boise, but only about twenty-eight percent of its workers live there. So the valley drives in every morning. That's why Interstate 84 matters so much. And transit is thin. Plan on two cars.

KID: Who lives there? Paint me the people.
GUIDE: Younger than the country. The median age is about thirty-seven, against about thirty-nine nationally. The metro is about eighty percent white. Hispanic families are the largest minority, about fifteen percent of the metro and about a quarter of Canyon County, where the farms are. Boise is also Idaho's main city for refugee resettlement. Families from dozens of countries have made homes there, though federal changes in 2025 cut new arrivals to a trickle.
KID: Church life?
GUIDE: Distinctive. A 2022 survey of Idahoans found about a third Protestant, a quarter Latter-day Saint, a quarter with no religion, and one in ten Catholic. In Ada County's congregations, Latter-day Saints and Catholics run close to even, each around three in ten, with nondenominational churches third. The Boise area has a bit of everything, including strong evangelical churches and a growing secular crowd downtown.
KID: Politics. Blue city, red state. True?
GUIDE: That's the shorthand. Here's the precise version. Idaho has leaned Republican since statehood. Every statewide office and every seat in Congress is held by a Republican. Ada County is Republican but competitive: Trump carried it by about four points in 2020 and about ten in 2024. Canyon County votes about seventy percent Republican. And the city of Boise itself leans Democratic.
KID: What happened in last week's election?
GUIDE: From the results reporting I have: Governor Brad Little won re-election, and Republicans held every statewide office and both seats in Congress. A ballot measure to create a right to abortion failed. An amendment making English the official state language passed. One that would have barred legalizing drugs by citizen initiative failed. I don't have the legislative results yet.
KID: For a business owner, where does the friction actually show up?
GUIDE: Between the statehouse and city hall. The Legislature regularly passes laws that limit what cities can do. Supporters say one set of statewide rules keeps things predictable and stops cities from overreaching. Critics say it takes decisions away from the people who have to live with them. Two examples, both from reporting I couldn't fully confirm. This spring the Legislature moved a bill to fine the city of Boise for flying a pride flag. And in 2025 the state created a tax credit, reported at five thousand dollars a student, for private-school and homeschool costs. Supporters call it parent choice; opponents worry it pulls money from public schools. It was challenged in court, and I don't know the outcome.
KID: So the practical lesson?
GUIDE: The state sets the base: low, flat taxes and light regulation. The city layers on what it's allowed to, and sometimes the two collide in public. Plan for both.
KID: And locals versus newcomers?
GUIDE: Every boom produces it. Long-timers feel the traffic and the prices. Newcomers want the things that drew them there. Valley surveys reportedly rank growth, transportation and affordable housing as the top concerns. The advice is old and it works. Listen more than you talk, and don't open with how you did it back in Washington.

KID: Okay. The money. Big picture first.
GUIDE: Three numbers. Unemployment in the Boise metro was three and a half percent in June. That's tight. The labor force actually shrank, by about seven-tenths of a percent from a year earlier. KID: And pay?
GUIDE: Here's the number that matters for anyone you recruit. Per person, income in the metro is about seventy-nine percent of the national level. Houses cost close to national prices, on incomes that aren't.

GUIDE: Here's the headline. From 2015 to 2025, construction jobs in the Boise metro went from about seventeen thousand to about thirty-seven thousand. More than doubled. All jobs together grew about forty percent. And in May of this year, construction was nearly two-thirds of Idaho's net new jobs.
KID: What's driving it?
GUIDE: Micron, first and biggest. In 2022 it announced a fifteen-billion-dollar fab in Boise, called the largest private investment in Idaho history.
