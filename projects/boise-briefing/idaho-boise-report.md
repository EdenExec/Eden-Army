# Idaho and Boise: a Briefing for a Family Moving to West Boise

Prepared by Scout for Eden Partner Group, 8 November 2026. Written to be told aloud, so it favours stories, people and numbers that stick. Every figure is linked to the page it came from.

**How to read the grades.** SETTLED means two or more sources agree, or a primary source states it. LIKELY means one good source, or sources that differ slightly. UNSURE means thin, conflicting or unverified. A dagger (†) after a link means the claim came from a search-result summary and I could not open the page itself. Treat † items as leads, not facts. ## Executive summary

- **Idaho is the fastest-growing state of the 2020s, and Boise is its engine.** Idaho grew 10.4% from April 2020 to July 2025, adding 190,610 people to reach 2,029,733, the fastest rate of any state in that window ([Post Register](https://www.postregister.com/news/local/idaho-s-population-growth-rate-second-in-nation-for-2025/article_18b40794-97f5-4454-b19c-4dc79251f017.html)). Ada and Canyon counties together hold an estimated 876,760 people in 2026, up from 726,072 at the 2020 Census ([Idaho Business Review, COMPASS data](https://idahobusinessreview.com/2026/05/18/treasure-valley-population-grows-150000-six-years/)). SETTLED.
- **The valley is in the middle of the largest construction build in Idaho history.** Micron is spending roughly $50 billion on two Boise memory fabs, with the first targeting wafers in mid-2027 and the second near the end of 2028 ([Construction Owners](https://www.constructionowners.com/news/microns-boise-megaproject-accelerates-as-first-chip-fab-nears-equipment-installation), [247WallSt](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/08/20/microns-50-billion-idaho-buildout-is-minting-millionaires-and-straining-local-infrastructure/)). Around it: a $1.1 billion St. Luke's tower, a Meta data center in Kuna above $800 million, a $700 million airport expansion ([EW](https://www.ewweb.com/electrical-economy/market-forecasts/article/55400775/boise-id-a-top-local-market-to-watch-in-2027)). For Eden this is the whole story: skilled construction talent is the scarce input.
- **The labor market is tight and shrinking at the edges.** Boise-Nampa unemployment was 3.5% in June 2026, and the labor force fell 0.7% from a year earlier ([Idaho Department of Labor](https://lmi.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/BoiseMSAProfile.pdf)). Construction was 64.5% of Idaho's net job gains in May 2026 ([IDOL Economic Situation Report](https://lmi.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/EconSitRep.pdf)). Wages have not kept pace with the cost of the place: metro per-capita income was $57,973 against $73,204 nationally in 2024 (79%).
- **Boise is a blue city in a deep-red state, and the Legislature keeps overruling it.** Ada County split about 50/46 for Trump in 2020 and 53/43 in 2024, while Canyon County next door goes about 72% Republican ([Ada County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_County,_Idaho), [Canyon County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canyon_County,_Idaho)). Fights over local control, flags, school funding and abortion recur every session. For a business owner, the state side is low tax and light regulation; the city side is progressive and slow.
- **The long-run risks are water, smoke and sprawl.** The winter of 2026 was a near-record snow drought across the West and Boise adopted a drought emergency ordinance on 1 July ([City of Boise](https://www.cityofboise.org/news/public-works/2026/july/city-of-boise-adopts-drought-ordinance/), [Boise State / The Conversation](https://www.boisestate.edu/news/2026/03/27/what-the-historic-snow-drought-means-for-the-west/)). Smoke from Oregon and Washington fires turned Treasure Valley air "unhealthy for everyone" in July ([Boise State Public Radio](https://www.boisestatepublicradio.org/environment/2026-07-22/deq-air-quality-wildfire-safety-advisory-idaho)). The upside is real: a diversifying economy, a state with a 5.3% flat income tax, and a valley that still has room.

## Part 1. Deep history

### The ground itself: lava, a flood and a river

Start with the geology, because everything in Boise follows from water. Southern Idaho sits on the Snake River Plain, a broad basin of old volcanic rock and river-laid sediment. The Boise River runs west out of the mountains, and the town sits where that river leaves the foothills and spreads across the flat.

About 14,500 years ago, by radiocarbon dating (roughly 17,400 calendar years), the biggest flood on the continent's late ice-age record tore down this plain. Lake Bonneville, a vast lake covering about 32,000 square miles in what is now northern Utah, breached a natural dam at Red Rock Pass. At peak it released about 33 million cubic feet per second, with flow speeds up to 70 mph, draining the top 351 feet of the lake, about 1,200 cubic miles of water. It carved the Snake River Canyon, formed Shoshone Falls, widened Hells Canyon and scattered "melon" boulders across the plain ([Bonneville flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonneville_flood)). SETTLED on the event; the source does not say how far the flood affected the Boise valley itself, which sits on a tributary, so do not tell it as "the flood made Boise."

People were here long before. A 2019 discovery at Cooper's Ferry in western Idaho points to human presence 15,300 to 16,600 years ago, and Wilson Butte Cave, excavated in 1959, yielded some of North America's oldest dated artifacts ([Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho)). LIKELY (single compiled source).

### The peoples of the Snake River country

The Boise valley was Northern Shoshone and Northern Paiute country. Idaho's tribes today are grouped into five: Kootenai, Coeur d'Alene and Nez Perce in the north; Shoshone and Northern Paiute in the south, with the Bannock (of Northern Paiute origin) living at Fort Hall ([Idaho Press, Indigenous Idaho](https://www.idahopress.com/250_anniversary/indigenous-idaho-native-lands-and-peoples/article_f503e48e-844a-4cdb-be14-1e91ee919a0b.html)). Shoshone tradition describes a yearly intertribal gathering where the Boise, Payette and Weiser rivers meet the Snake, opening the fishing season and trading shells, hides, dried meat and arrowheads. That is a good detail for the podcast: the confluence west of Boise was a trading fair long before it was a farm.

What came next is the hard part. Gold rushes beginning around 1860 pushed settlers into this country, and Native people were "forced onto reservation lands" (same Idaho Press piece). The sequence, from the best sources I could open:

- **1854**: the Ward Massacre, in which Shoshone attacked an emigrant train within about 20 miles of Old Fort Boise ([Fort Boise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Boise)).
- **1862 to 1863**: mining rushes in the Boise Basin (1862) and the Owyhee canyonlands (1863) brought thousands of settlers into Native territory, and raids and retaliation escalated ([Snake War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snake_War)).
- **1864 to 1868**: the Snake War, fought across Oregon, Nevada, California and Idaho Territory against Northern Paiute, Bannock and Western Shoshone bands. About 1,762 men were killed, wounded or captured, which that article calls statistically the deadliest of the western Indian Wars (same source; the "deadliest" ranking is the article's own claim, so LIKELY).
- **10 October 1864**: a Treaty of Fort Boise was concluded but the U.S. Senate never ratified it ([Boise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise,_Idaho)).
- **1867 to 1869**: the Fort Hall Reservation was set by executive order in June 1867, with the 1868 Fort Bridger Treaty and a Grant executive order in 1869 placing the Bannock and associated Shoshone bands there. Its original land base of about 1.8 million acres later shrank to roughly 1.2 million ([Shoshone-Bannock Tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoshone-Bannock_Tribes)).
- **1869**: the military removed Shoshone and Bannock people from the Boise area, a move the Boise article calls "Idaho's Trail of Tears" ([Boise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise,_Idaho)). That label is Wikipedia's wording, not a settled scholarly term, so tell it with care. LIKELY.

Today the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes at Fort Hall have about 6,000 enrolled members, a seven-member elected business council, and treaty fishing and hunting rights upheld by the Ninth Circuit in 1994. They hold a festival and powwow every August that reached its 60th edition in 2025 ([Shoshone-Bannock Tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoshone-Bannock_Tribes)). The Shoshone-Paiute Tribes at Duck Valley, on the Idaho-Nevada line, are the other southern Idaho nation; their reservation was set by executive order in 1877 and the bands were federally recognized as a single tribe in 1934 (†[Duck Valley Indian Reservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duck_Valley_Indian_Reservation); I did not open it). The Nez Perce, whose aboriginal territory covered about 13 million acres of north-central Idaho and neighbouring states (†[Idaho Supreme Court, Tribal History](https://isc.idaho.gov/node/70)), fought the 1877 Nez Perce War, which began at White Bird Canyon in June 1877 ([Idaho Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho_Territory)).

I could not find a source I could open on the Boise Valley Shoshone as a distinct group, or on a tribal-run source for the Boise area. If Atlas wants more here, the tribes' own sites and the Idaho State Historical Society are the next stop. UNSURE.

### Lewis and Clark, fur traders and the first Fort Boise

The Lewis and Clark expedition crossed Idaho in 1805 on the way west and again in 1806 on the way back. In the spring of 1806 the Corps waited on the Clearwater for the snow to melt, and Lewis's dog Seaman was stolen on 11 April and recovered soon after ([Lewis and Clark Expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_and_Clark_Expedition), [Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho)). Note: they went nowhere near Boise. Their route was the Lemhi and Lolo country in the north. The Boise valley was bypassed.

The fur trade found Boise next. The Hudson's Bay Company built the first Fort Boise in the fall of 1834 under Thomas McKay, to counter the American Fort Hall. François Payette ran it from 1835 to 1844, staffed largely by Hawaiian workers, and built a second fort in 1838 near the Boise and Snake confluence, about five miles northwest of today's Parma. A flood damaged it in 1853; the army judged it indefensible; it was abandoned in 1854 ([Fort Boise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Boise)). SETTLED. The Hawaiian workers (called "Kanakas" in the period) are a fun, true detail.

The Oregon Trail ran past it. About 400,000 settlers used the trail and its offshoots over a route of roughly 2,170 miles; traffic peaked 1846 to 1869 and collapsed after the transcontinental railroad opened in 1869 ([Oregon Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oregon_Trail)). Fort Boise was one of three Hudson's Bay posts, with Fort Hall and Fort Nez Perce, that gave desperate emigrants aid.

### 1862: gold in the Boise Basin

The state's own historical society tells the gold story best. On 2 August 1862, George Grimes, Moses Splawn and a small party found rich placers on Grimes Creek. Grimes was killed within days, in a clash with Native people, by most accounts ([Idaho State Historical Society, Reference Series 166](https://history.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/0166.pdf), †[KTVB](https://ktvb.com/article/news/local/208/how-grimes-creek-got-its-name-today-in-1862/277-55882661-0a09-4659-abeb-af9e015008ee)). A strong party out of Walla Walla founded Pioneerville and Idaho City on 7 October 1862. By January 1863 Placerville had about a thousand people and 4,000 miners were in the basin.

The numbers are the story. Even after the summer crowd thinned in fall 1863, the Boise Basin held about 16,000 people, and more than 6,000 lived in Idaho City, which the society says surpassed Portland to become the largest community in the Pacific Northwest. Miners dug eighty miles of ditches by 1863 to feed water to the claims; in spring 1864 five or six hundred hopefuls arrived each week and more than thirty claim fires were visible from Idaho City at night. SETTLED (Idaho State Historical Society, primary-adjacent).

There is a political detail worth telling. By 1864 the Boise Basin and Boise Valley had become "southern strongholds," with enough Confederate refugees from Missouri that Missouri-Kansas border bitterness was brought to Idaho; Idaho City saw "violent partisan clashes during the height of the Civil War" (same source). Boise started life as a service town for the mines, at the crossing of the Oregon Trail with the roads to Idaho City and Silver City.

### 1863: the army arrives and a city is born

On 4 July 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, the Union Army founded a new Fort Boise about fifty miles up the Boise River from the old fort. Major Pinkney Lugenbeel chose the site, which became the city. It was built to protect Oregon Trail travelers after the old fort closed. The army stayed 49 years and left in 1912; the site later served World War I veterans and tuberculosis patients, then the VA, and part of it became Fort Boise Park in 1950 ([Fort Boise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Boise)). SETTLED.

Idaho Territory was organized by Congress on 3 or 4 March 1863 (the sources differ on the day) and at first covered present Idaho, Montana and most of Wyoming ([Idaho Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho_Territory), [ISHS 344](https://history.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/0344_Location-of-Idahos-Territorial-Capital.pdf)).

On the name: the origin of "Boise" is genuinely uncertain. One story has Captain Bonneville's French-speaking guide crying "Les bois!" (the woods!) at the sight of trees along the river; another says French-Canadian trappers in the 1820s called it "la rivière boisée" ([Boise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise,_Idaho)). UNSURE.

### The capital fight: how Boise took the capital from Lewiston

This is the best story in Idaho's early history, and the Idaho State Historical Society tells it in a wonderfully dry document ([Reference Series 344](https://history.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/0344_Location-of-Idahos-Territorial-Capital.pdf)). In outline:

- The first governor, William Wallace, set up in Lewiston in the north in July 1863, where the population had been. But gold in the Boise Basin had swung the center of gravity south. The territory's 1863 count showed the south far ahead, and by the second legislature in 1864 the south outnumbered the north 18,997 to 2,790.
- Northern members tried to dodge by asking Congress to cut off a new northern territory. The south had the votes, defeated that, and made Boise the capital. Boise's date as capital, the society says, is **24 December 1864**.
- Lewiston lawyers sued, arguing the whole session was illegal because nobody could say what day it should have convened. The territorial governor, Caleb Lyon, left while "out hunting ducks," leaving his private secretary to be the entire executive department. Lewiston posted an armed guard over the territorial seal and archives.
- Then the new territorial secretary, C. Dewitt Smith, arrived on 2 March 1865 with money and the ability to call federal troops from Fort Lapwai. He went for a ride one afternoon, came back the next day with soldiers, and left for Boise with the seal and as many papers as he could carry. Lewiston threw the rest of the papers into the Lewiston jail. Smith reached Boise on **14 April 1865**.
- The territorial supreme court later upheld the legislature's second session, so Boise had been the capital since 24 December 1864.

Other references say Boise became capital in 1866, and one says it was by a one-vote margin on the court ([Idaho Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho_Territory)). The historical society says the court's order was recorded but its opinion was not. I'd tell the 1864 and 1865 version, which is the primary source, and note the 1866 date appears in reference works. LIKELY on the dates; SETTLED on the sequence.

The north-south split in Idaho dates from this fight (my framing, not a sourced claim). (A BoiseDev piece on the "two capital cities" exists but I could not open it: †[BoiseDev](https://boisedev.com/news/2022/11/28/boise-lewiston-capitals-2/).)

### Territory to statehood, 1870 to 1890

Territorial Boise was small. The census counted 995 people in 1870, 1,899 in 1880 and 2,311 in 1890 ([Boise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise,_Idaho)). The U.S. Assay Office (1871 to 1872) bought gold and silver; the territorial penitentiary opened in 1872; electric power came on 4 July 1887; an electric streetcar ran by 1891. The Oregon Short Line Railroad reached the valley in 1883 ([Treasure Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_Valley)), and Nampa became the rail hub.

Territorial politics were rough. Former Southern Democrats held sway in the legislature and fought Republican governors; in 1867 Governor David Ballard asked troops at Fort Boise for protection from the legislature. In 1882 Latter-day Saint voters were disenfranchised over polygamy, and in 1887 President Cleveland pocket-vetoed a bill to give northern Idaho to Washington Territory ([Idaho Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho_Territory)). Idaho's constitution was approved by voters on 5 November 1889 and President Benjamin Harrison signed statehood on **3 July 1890**, making Idaho the 43rd state. Territorial population: 14,999 (1870), 32,610 (1880), 88,548 (1890).

Chinese laborers were a large part of the gold-rush population. The 1870 census counted 1,751 Chinese residents in Idaho City alone (same source). Boise had a Chinatown too, which was later demolished (see the urban renewal section below).

### Water makes the desert: irrigation and the Reclamation era

Boise exists because of ditches. The Idaho State Historical Society's irrigation history says that in 1863, right after the gold rush, "all the easily watered lands immediately adjacent to Boise River were taken up and farmed," and by 1864 canal companies were forming. Private companies built the big canals first (the Ridenbaugh and the New York Canal). When private companies failed across the West, Congress passed the **Carey Act of 1894**, letting each arid state take up to a million acres to sell in 160-acre lots through approved canal companies. Idaho benefited far more than any other state: about **60% of all Carey Act lands irrigated in the United States are in Idaho**. Twin Falls, with Milner Dam bringing water by 1905, is the showcase ([ISHS Reference Series 260, Irrigation in Idaho](https://history.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/0260_Irrigation-in-Idaho-1.pdf)). SETTLED.

The **Reclamation Act of 1902** created the federal Reclamation Service (now the Bureau of Reclamation). The Boise Project and the Minidoka projects grew from it. The great early dam was **Arrowrock**, on the Boise River upriver from town, begun around 1911 to 1912 and dedicated 4 October 1915. At 350 feet it was the tallest dam in the world until Switzerland's Schräh dam (366 feet) in 1924 ([Arrowrock Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrowrock_Dam)). The Idaho State Historical Society says "about twenty years," which conflicts with nine years; the number depends on which record is meant, so say "for years" on air. LIKELY. Lucky Peak Dam, below Arrowrock, came later. Further south the Owyhee Project (Owyhee Dam, completed 16 September 1932) put more than 111,000 acres under irrigation by 1965 ([Treasure Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_Valley)).

Note the lesson that matters for the future: the valley's farmland, and now its subdivisions, run on 19th- and early-20th-century water rights.

### The Basques

Boise has one of the largest Basque communities in the United States, and the story starts with silver. Basques were drawn to Idaho by the silver mines at Silver City; those who did not mine ranched and sold lamb and beef to the miners. Later, from the 1940s to about 1970, the Western Range Association recruited sheepherders directly from Spain ([Basque Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_Americans)). Pioneers like Jose Bengoechea and John Achabal owned property by 1912. Boise is a sister city to Gernika (since 1990), and the long-time Secretary of State Pete Cenarrusa and his successor Ben Ysursa are Basque-American Republicans; former mayor Dave Bieter speaks Basque.

How many? The numbers conflict. The Wikipedia Basque Americans article gives 3,573 Basque-Americans in Boise and 8,196 Idaho residents reporting Basque ancestry; the Treasure Valley article says over 15,000 Basques came to the valley ([Treasure Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_Valley)). The Census never separated Basques cleanly, so early counts are unreliable. UNSURE on totals; SETTLED that the community is large and central to the city.

The Basque Block is on Grove Street downtown, between Capitol Boulevard and 6th Street ([Boise State Public Radio on Jaialdi](https://www.boisestatepublicradio.org/news/2025-06-26/jaialdi-basque-idaho-festival-san-inazio)). The festival Jaialdi is held every five years; the 2020 edition was postponed by COVID and held 29 July to 3 August 2025, the first since 2015 (same source; †[Visit Boise](https://visitboise.com/events/jaialdi-2025/) puts attendance at "upwards of 30,000" for a past festival).

### Railroads, streetcars and a small capital

Boise's Union Pacific Depot dates to 1925 ([Boise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise,_Idaho)). The Interurban rail line connected valley towns between 1908 and 1928 ([Meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian,_Idaho)). Interstate 84 eventually replaced all of that. The interstate was first numbered I-82 in 1957, renumbered I-80N in 1958, built in sections from 1965 to 1968 and renamed I-84 on 1 May 1980 ([Interstate 84 in Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_84_in_Idaho)).

Boise's population stagnated between wars. It was 21,393 in 1920 and 21,544 in 1930, then 26,130 in 1940 ([Boise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise,_Idaho)).

### World War II: Gowen Field and Minidoka

Boise's airport had the longest runway in the country in 1938 (8,800 feet, a WPA project). The U.S. Army Air Forces leased it as a training base for B-17 and B-24 bomber crews, with more than 6,000 men stationed there. The field was named Gowen Field on 23 July 1941, after Lt. Paul R. Gowen, a Caldwell native killed in a 1938 bomber crash in Panama. The Idaho Air National Guard still flies from it: the 124th Fighter Wing operates A-10s ([Boise Airport / Gowen Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gowen_Field)). SETTLED.

The hard chapter of the war sits two hours southeast. The Minidoka War Relocation Center, near Hunt in Jerome County, operated from 1942 to 1945 and formally closed 28 October 1945. At its peak it held 9,397 people, and the National Park Service says it commemorates "more than 13,000" Japanese Americans, most from Oregon, Washington and Alaska. 844 men from the camp volunteered or were drafted for military service. The site became a national monument on 17 January 2001 and a National Historic Site in 2008 ([Minidoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minidoka_National_Historic_Site)). SETTLED. Many Japanese American families stayed in the Treasure Valley after the war to farm.

### The companies Boise gave the world

This is the "why does Boise matter" section, and also Eden's world.

- **Morrison-Knudsen (MK).** Harry Morrison and Morris Knudsen met in 1905 on an Idaho irrigation project, began working together in 1912 and incorporated in 1923. MK was part of the "Six Companies" that built Hoover Dam (1932 to 1935), and helped build the San Francisco-Oakland Bay Bridge and the Trans-Alaska Pipeline. In 1941 Japanese forces captured about 1,200 workers, many from MK, on Midway and Wake. After the war MK did St. Lawrence Seaway locks, the DEW Line, Minuteman silos, Kennedy Space Center, over 100 dams. Transit-car losses and the ouster of CEO William Agee in 1995 pushed it into bankruptcy in 1996. Washington Group bought it for $380 million; URS bought that in 2007; AECOM bought URS in 2014; the last Boise positions were eliminated in 2015 ([Morrison-Knudsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrison-Knudsen)). SETTLED. Boise's construction culture, and a good chunk of its contractor families and old project managers, descend from MK.
- **Albertsons.** Joe Albertson founded it on 21 July 1939 in Boise. It went public in 1959 and still has its headquarters in Boise. It completed the Safeway merger in January 2015 (about $9.2 billion) and a Kroger deal worth about $25 billion was blocked by a federal judge in December 2024 and terminated on 11 December 2024 ([Albertsons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertsons)). SETTLED.
- **J. R. Simplot.** Born 1909 in Iowa, in Idaho from 1910, left school after eighth grade at 14. He began by feeding hogs with potatoes and horse meat. By World War II he was the largest fresh-potato shipper in the nation. A 1967 handshake deal with McDonald's founder Ray Kroc made him the chain's frozen-fry supplier; by 2005 he supplied more than half of McDonald's fries. He bankrolled early Micron and held about 20% by 1994. He died on 25 May 2008 at 99, the oldest billionaire on the Forbes 400 ([J. R. Simplot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._Simplot)). He also bought Bogus Basin's lifts in 1953 and leased them back, keeping the ski area afloat ([Bogus Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogus_Basin)). SETTLED.
- **Boise Cascade.** Formed in 1957 by merging Boise Payette Lumber with Cascade Lumber of Yakima, Washington. The 1960s turned it into a conglomerate (even a cruise line and a motor-home maker) and overextension and a falling stock price forced CEO Robert Hansberger out in 1972. Today it makes engineered wood and distributes building products from Boise, and is listed on the NYSE as BCC ([Boise Cascade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise_Cascade)).
- **Hewlett-Packard and Micron.** HP bought a 150-acre site in northwest Boise in 1973; Micron was founded in 1978 ([Treasure Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_Valley)). Micron was founded by Ward Parkinson, Joe Parkinson, Dennis Wilson and Doug Pitman, funded by local businessmen and then Simplot. It began making 64K DRAM in 1981 and went public in 1984 ([Micron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micron_Technology)).

### Hard chapters, told plainly

**1955: the Boise "witch hunt."** Three men were arrested on 31 October 1955 for sexual acts with young men, and the Idaho Statesman, then Boise's only daily, ran a 3 November editorial headlined "Crush the Monster." The investigation widened into a hunt for a supposed "homosexual underground." Some 1,500 people were questioned (another section of the source gives 1,472), sixteen men were charged and fifteen sentenced. A private detective compiled a list of about 500 suspected homosexuals. One man, Ralph Cooper, got a life sentence and served nine years; Joe Moore got seven. The Statesman reversed itself on 20 November, calling for psychiatric treatment rather than prison. It wound down in January 1957. Time magazine covered it; John Gerassi's 1966 book *The Boys of Boise* argues it served an elite's effort to control the city and halted when it got near elite families ([Boise homosexuality scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise_homosexuality_scandal)). The facts above are SETTLED as to counts and dates, though the source itself notes the figures vary. Gerassi's motive theory is contested; say "Gerassi argued." The episode still shades how some Boiseans see their city, and it recurred in the 2007 Larry Craig arrest coverage.

**Removal of the Shoshone and Bannock** (1869) and the **Snake War**: covered above.

**Minidoka**: covered above. Idaho was a place of imprisonment as well as refuge.

**The 1960s to 1980s: tearing down downtown.** Boise's own version of urban renewal is a local sore point. Downtown leaders wanted a regional shopping mall; the Boise Redevelopment Agency (predecessor of today's Capital City Development Corp) tore down buildings including the Pierce Building, Chinatown and the original City Hall. The plan would have razed the Union Block, Alexander, Idaho, McCarty and Fidelity buildings and everything between Bannock, 9th, and Grove streets and Capitol Boulevard. By about 1980 the urban core was "largely deserted." Preservation Idaho formed in the early 1970s to stop demolitions; the Statesman hosted public forums that drew more than 1,600 people; in the 1983 and 1985 elections voters chose leaders (including Brent Coles and Dirk Kempthorne) who opposed the downtown mall and backed a suburban one. Boise Towne Square, in west Boise, began construction in late 1986 ([CCDC, Boise's Mall Saga](https://ccdcboise.com/top-50-stories-1965-1988-boises-mall-saga/)). SETTLED. Practical meaning for the family: the big mall near your new home exists because of that fight. An MIT thesis says nearly eight city blocks were demolished from the late 1960s to mid-1970s with federal urban renewal funds (†[MIT thesis listing](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/32192)). The nickname "the Boise Hole" for the long-empty downtown lot is something I could not verify in a source I could open. UNSURE; treat it as local lore until Atlas or Kev confirms.

The 8th and Main tower that now fills the old Eastman Building site opened in early 2014 as Idaho's tallest building (same CCDC source).

### Sprawl, farmland and infrastructure

Farmland is the raw material for growth. The Treasure Valley article notes that the metro's growth is "urbanizing undeveloped and agricultural land." Meridian began as an irrigated dairy and orchard town, with a creamery that ran from 1929 to 1970 ([Meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian,_Idaho)). Expect to see the transition from fields to subdivisions on the road to Star, Kuna and Middleton.

**Roads.** I-84 is the valley's spine: about 276 miles in Idaho; average daily traffic in 2018 was 139,113 vehicles at the I-184 split in western Boise versus 9,552 near Sweetzer Summit. The state widened Nampa to eight lanes in 2019 to 2021 (about $150 million) and four miles through Caldwell to six lanes in August 2023 (about $300 million); a 2009 and 2011 project widened six miles between Nampa and Meridian to eight lanes (about $113 million) ([Interstate 84 in Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_84_in_Idaho)).

**Transit.** Transit is thin. Reporting I could only see as search summaries says Valley Regional Transit has no dedicated state funding and cannot raise its own taxes, that ridership fell from 1,345,418 (FY2017) to 818,569 (FY2021), and that Boise voted in a past year to move toward cutting its city funding (†[KTVB](https://ktvb.com/article/news/local/vallley-regional-transit-bus-routes-new-changes-funding-for-buses/277-7196bb23-f696-40c7-85bc-03cd0e7060df), †[Idaho Press](https://www.idahopress.com/news/local/valley-regional-waiting-for-ridership-numbers-to-return-to-normal/article_5282443a-942d-564c-917e-d77b20efc085.html)). LIKELY. For a family in 83713, plan on two cars.

**The regional plan.** COMPASS, the valley's metropolitan planning organization, published a Communities in Motion 2050 plan; its 2019 forecast was about 1,075,000 for Ada and Canyon by 2050, and a newer forecast says the Treasure Valley rises from about 823,000 in 2024 to 1.3 million in 2055 (†[COMPASS FAQ](https://compassidaho.org/faqs/), †[search summary of CIM 2050 documents](https://cim2050.compassidaho.org/Demographics.pdf)). Note that the valley blew past the old forecast's early path: 876,760 already in 2026. A follow-up plan (CIM 2055) is due by December 2027.

### Religion

Idaho's faith map is distinctive: in the 2022 PRRI American Values Survey, Protestants were 37%, Latter-day Saints 24%, the religiously unaffiliated 26% and Catholics 9% ([Religion in Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Idaho)). Plain-spoken version: southeast Idaho (Idaho Falls, Rexburg, Pocatello) is heavily LDS; the Boise area has a large LDS community alongside strong evangelical and Catholic communities and a growing secular, progressive downtown. Idaho's first organized non-Native communities were Mormon pioneer settlements in 1860 ([Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho)). The Meridian and Eagle belts lean more church-going and more Republican than Boise's North End.

### Where talent is scarce

Evidence, with dates:

- **Early 2024 projection (Yahoo / Idaho industry coverage).** Wayne Hammon, CEO of the Idaho Associated General Contractors, said Micron alone could need "4,000 construction workers" at peak. Joe Jackson of Engineered Structures Inc. estimated about 2,000 licensed electricians in Idaho, roughly 75% in the Treasure Valley, with the mega-projects needing over 1,200 at peak. - **2026 evidence.** The Micron CEO says trade-skill shortage is limiting the pace. Construction led Idaho's job gains in May 2026. The metro's single-family permits are steady and multifamily permits dropped (EW). Statewide, a "historically low" 62.0% labor force participation rate means fewer people to hire.

My read (analysis, not a sourced fact): the scarce talent is, in order, licensed electricians and instrumentation/controls trades; pipefitters, welders and mechanical trades (cleanroom process piping); superintendents and project managers who have run $100 million-plus or industrial/mission-critical projects; estimators and preconstruction leads; and safety, quality and commissioning managers. The commercial real estate side (brokerage, development, asset management) is thinner and smaller, and more tied to housing, retail, and the local developers.

One more structural point, again my analysis: the construction peak of the Micron build is likely to arrive sometime between now and the second fab's completion in late 2028. Recruiters who pre-position relationships with the superintendents and project executives of the contractors now on the Micron, Meta and hospital jobs will be the ones placing them when the work winds down, and with the next wave of work elsewhere (Micron's New York sites, other data centers).

## Part 5. Culture

### Boise State and the blue turf

Boise State installed its blue field ("The Blue") in 1986, the first major college program with a non-green surface. The signature moment is the **2007 Fiesta Bowl**, in which Boise State beat Oklahoma 43-42 in overtime after the 2006 season. Chris Petersen coached 2006 to 2013 and went 92-12. The stadium (Albertsons Stadium, called Bronco Stadium until 2014) seats 32,423. The team went to the 2024-25 College Football Playoff as the No. 3 seed, got a first-round bye, and lost 31-14 to Penn State in the Fiesta Bowl quarterfinal; running back Ashton Jeanty was the first unanimous All-American in program history and won the Maxwell and Doak Walker awards that year. Boise State left the Mountain West and joined the Pac-12 on **1 July 2026** ([Boise State Broncos football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise_State_Broncos_football), [Boise State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise_State_University)). SETTLED. (The Wikipedia page gives conflicting records for current coach Spencer Danielson, 30-9 or 31-9, and the all-time record in two forms; do not read out records.) Boise State won the 1980 NCAA Division I-AA national championship.

### The Basque Block and Jaialdi

See Part 1. Add food: if Atlas wants a detail to anchor it, the Basque Block is where Boiseans go for croquetas, lamb and Spanish-style dishes, a living survival of the sheepherder boardinghouse tradition (the boardinghouse detail is general knowledge; I could not source it this session).

### Treefort and the arts

Treefort Music Fest began in 2012, founded by producer Lori Shandro (after her husband died in a 2009 plane crash) through her company The Duck Club, a B Corp since 2015. It started with 137 bands and about 3,000 people on each of three days; attendance reached 18,000 in 2017 and 24,000 in 2018; the 2026 festival booked over 500 artists; and the festival injected an estimated $15.8 million into the local economy in 2025 (up from $11 million in 2023) ([Treefort Music Fest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treefort_Music_Fest)). SETTLED.

### The river, the Greenbelt and the foothills

The Boise River Greenbelt runs more than 20 miles from Lucky Peak Dam to just past Eagle Road in Eagle (over 30 miles counting both banks and spurs). Its origins are 1960s city planning: a 1962 comprehensive plan proposed a continuous public green belt; three donated parcels in 1967 started it; the Parks Commission adopted a plan in 1968; a 1971 ordinance required a 70-foot setback for buildings and parking ([Boise Greenbelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise_Greenbelt)). This is the single best "Boise made a good decision" story, and it came from the same era as the urban renewal mistakes.

Bogus Basin, 16 road miles north of downtown, opened in December 1942 with a 500-foot rope tow. It is a nonprofit, sits partly on Boise National Forest land, has 2,600 skiable acres, 1,790 feet of vertical, and 165 acres of night skiing; its summit is 7,582 feet at Shafer Butte. Snowfall has run below the historic 200 to 250 inches a year since 2011 ([Bogus Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogus_Basin)). With 2026's record-low snowpack, the season was hard on it (the Boise State piece notes many Western resorts closed early); I did not find a Bogus Basin specific 2026 report. Floating the river in summer, hunting and fishing in the Boise National Forest and beyond, and the Foothills trail network are the daily outdoor culture. I did not source specifics on the river float, food, craft beer or the "City of Trees" story this session; the origin of the Boise name (above) and the "trees along the river" imagery is the closest source-supported link. UNSURE on specifics.

### Climate, so you dress right

Boise is semi-arid with four distinct seasons: about 12 inches of precipitation a year, about 18 inches of snow a year, 100-degree days about nine times a year, and records of 111°F (1898 and 1960) and -28°F (1888) ([Boise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise,_Idaho)).

## Part 6. What to watch: 5, 10 and 25+ years

For each, I give the evidence first and my judgment second. Judgment is labeled and is not a forecast I can source.

### Water and drought

**Evidence.** The valley's farms and subdivisions run on stored mountain snow. Winter 2026 was an extreme snow drought: of roughly 70 Western river basins, only five were at or above median snow water equivalent for the date, mostly in the Yellowstone region; Boise reached the low 80s in mid-March during a heat dome; Boise State professor Lejo Flores wrote "winter 2026 was the year the snow never came" ([Boise State / The Conversation](https://www.boisestate.edu/news/2026/03/27/what-the-historic-snow-drought-means-for-the-west/)). The City of Boise adopted a drought emergency ordinance on 1 July 2026, with phased responses from voluntary to mandatory watering limits, tied to the U.S. Drought Index for Ada County. It applies only to the public drinking-water system, not to private wells, canals or irrigation providers; the water provider Veolia reports summer demand above 90 million gallons a day versus 25 million in winter ([City of Boise](https://www.cityofboise.org/news/public-works/2026/july/city-of-boise-adopts-drought-ordinance/)). NRCS data cited in search summaries put the Boise basin at 44% of normal snowpack on 1 April, but reservoirs were about 94% full in May and the year was not among the worst on record because of storage (†[NRCS April 2026 outlook](https://nrcs-prod.azureedge.us/sites/default/files/2026-04/borid426.pdf) (too large to open), †[search summaries]). LIKELY.

**Judgment.** Reservoirs bought time in 2026. Two or three such winters in a row would be a different story. Water rights, not water supply as such, are the political fight to come, because the senior rights belong to irrigators and canal companies while the new houses need drinking water. Watch: the Boise reservoir system's fall carryover, winter snowpack on 1 April 2027, any further phases of the city ordinance.

### Wildfire and smoke

**Evidence.** In July 2026 smoke from Central Oregon and Washington fires pushed Treasure Valley air to "unhealthy" for everyone, with a DEQ advisory covering Ada, Canyon, Gem, Payette and Washington counties and a ban on outdoor burning ([Boise State Public Radio](https://www.boisestatepublicradio.org/environment/2026-07-22/deq-air-quality-wildfire-safety-advisory-idaho)). Search summaries say the valley briefly had the worst air in the country, and that 2024 had the most "unhealthy" days in EPA data back to 1999 (†). LIKELY. **Judgment:** plan for two to three weeks of smoky air each late summer, an air purifier in each bedroom, and a family habit of checking AirNow.

### Growth limits, housing and the state-local fight

**Evidence.** Growth has slowed from 3% (2021) to about 1.4% (2025) but is still triple the national rate. Ada and Canyon are already ahead of earlier forecasts. The Legislature is limiting what cities can do. **Judgment:** the valley will keep adding about 10,000 to 15,000 people a year for the foreseeable future, mostly in Canyon County and the western edge of Ada (Star, Kuna, Middleton, Caldwell, Nampa), because that is where land and entry-level housing are. Meridian becomes the second city in fact as well as name. Watch: annexation fights, impact fees, the multi-family permit slump, and whether the Legislature preempts more city zoning.

### Micron and the semiconductor bet

**Evidence.** Micron's Boise investment is more than any other private bet in Idaho's history, with first output in 2027. Memory chips are cyclical, and Micron's stock and capital plans swing with the cycle; the company has also moved volumes between sites (it delayed New York, accelerated Idaho's second fab and reallocated CHIPS funds, per a trade summary †[3DTested](https://www.3dtested.com/pc-components/dram/microns-new-york-chipmaking-fabs-by-five-years-but-accelerates-second-fab-in-idaho-and-reallocates-chips-act-funding)). **Judgment:** the risks are a memory down-cycle, a trade or tariff shock, and a shift in AI demand; the opportunity is a permanent cluster of suppliers (gases, chemicals, equipment service) that make Boise a semiconductor town the way Austin and Phoenix became. The 5-year view is strong construction and then a hiring wave for operations; the 10-year view depends on ID1 and ID2 running full.

### Infrastructure

I-84 widening continues in pieces. Without dedicated transit funding, expect more lanes and few trains. Power is a quiet constraint: a data center, two fabs and a hospital tower all need electricity, and the CEO flagged "enhanced energy infrastructure." Utilities and water treatment sit inside the $50 billion Micron number (Construction Owners). Watch Idaho Power's capacity plans (I did not source them).

### Diversifying the economy

Idaho's economy is more than potatoes. The state profile shows healthcare and education now the metro's largest sector by jobs (89,847), construction is second-fastest-growing, and manufacturing (30,013) is led by Micron (IDOL profile). The risk is concentration: construction was 64.5% of job gains in one recent month; Micron, health systems and state government dominate the employer list. The opportunity is that there are now enough tech, finance and professional services jobs (55,107 in professional and business services) to support a real commercial office market.

### Climate

Boise is projected to be hotter and to see less winter snow; the March 2026 heat dome is the sort of event to expect more often. I did not source a regional climate projection this session. UNSURE.

### Politics

Expect more clashes: a Republican supermajority-style Legislature (Democrats hold about 15 of 105 seats by the Capital Sun summary †), a Democratic city, and an attorney general (Raúl Labrador) willing to use his office. For a business owner, the state's tax and regulation climate is friendly, and the city's permitting and politics are not. I would plan for both.

### Risks and opportunities for a business owner and a family

Risks: housing cost relative to income; wildfire smoke; water; exposure to one anchor employer; rapid change in schools and roads.
Opportunities: Eden's own market. Construction employment doubled in ten years, a $50 billion build is underway, talent is scarce, wages are rising, and the executive layer (project executives, regional VPs, preconstruction and operations heads, development leads) is thin enough that a specialist search firm with a Boise presence has real room. One caution, from the data: median household income is below the national figure, so the best clients are the national and regional contractors and industrial owners moving in, not only the local firms.

### How locals talk about the valley (general knowledge, not from opened sources; UNSURE)

- **"The Valley"** or **"the Treasure Valley"** means Boise, Meridian, Eagle, Nampa, Caldwell, Kuna, Star and surroundings. "Ada County" is the Boise/Meridian/Eagle/Kuna/Star side; "Canyon County" is Nampa/Caldwell/Middleton.
- Neighbourhoods Boiseans name: the **North End** (older, tree-lined, near the foothills, a progressive stronghold), **the Bench** (the raised flat south of downtown), **Hyde Park**, **downtown**, **Garden City**, and the **Foothills**. The west side (where you are) is sometimes described as "West Boise" or just "out by Meridian."
- **Pronunciation**: Boise is "BOY-see," not "BOY-zee." Meridian is Meh-RID-ee-un. Idaho is "EYE-duh-ho." The town of Boise's name origin story is the French "les bois" (see Part 1).
- **Time zone**: Boise and southern Idaho are on Mountain Time, one hour ahead of Vancouver. Idaho spans two time zones (Wikipedia, Idaho). Northern Idaho is Pacific.

### Things a newcomer gets wrong

1. **Treating Boise like Idaho.** Boise is the exception: bluer, richer, more educated and more diverse than most of the state. Rural Idaho and the LDS-heavy southeast are different places.
2. **Assuming a Washington sales and income tax picture.** Idaho taxes income at 5.3% flat and sales at 6%; Washington has no wage income tax. Ask a CPA before your first Idaho paycheck or return.
3. **Underestimating the summer.** Hot (100-degree days about nine a year), dry, smoky some weeks. Irrigation water comes from canals and ditches; do not fill a ditch or a canal-side fence line without checking, and keep children and pets away from canals. (Canal safety is general knowledge; confirm with your irrigation district.)
4. **Assuming transit.** Plan on two vehicles.
5. **Starting a business and ignoring the Legislature.** What Boise's council passes can be undone in Boise's statehouse.
6. **Talking about "Californians" or "the way we did it in Washington."** The resentment is real. Lead with curiosity.
7. **Misjudging the commute.** I-84 and the arterials back up; Boise is the job center, and many suburban residents drive across the valley. Test your drive at 8 a.m., not at 10.
